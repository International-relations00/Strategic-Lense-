Why the Baltic States Are Rewriting NATO's Eastern Doctrine

A close read of recent defense posture shifts suggests smaller member states are no longer waiting for consensus — they're setting the agenda themselves.

By Zubair 12 min read Published Aug 28, 2026 Updated Aug 29, 2026

For most of the alliance's post-Cold War history, doctrine on the eastern flank was set in Brussels and ratified, more or less quietly, by the states closest to the border. That sequence has started to reverse.

Over the past eighteen months, Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania have moved from requesting NATO posture changes to pre-committing national resources ahead of alliance-level agreement — effectively presenting Brussels with facts on the ground rather than waiting for consensus to form.

Smaller states are no longer just consumers of alliance strategy. In this case, they're authoring it.

What changed

Three developments explain the shift: sustained defense-spending increases well above the 2% guideline, bilateral basing arrangements negotiated directly with individual NATO members rather than through the alliance structure, and a coordinated diplomatic push at the ministerial level that bypassed the usual working-group sequencing.

The spending signal

Defense spending as a share of GDP across the three Baltic states now sits meaningfully above the alliance average — a figure that matters less for its raw size than for what it buys in negotiating leverage inside NATO's internal politics.

Why this matters beyond the Baltics

If smaller member states can successfully set precedent through unilateral action, it changes the incentive structure for the rest of the eastern flank — and raises real questions about how much "alliance doctrine" is still authored collectively versus adopted after the fact.
