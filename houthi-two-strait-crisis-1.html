How a "Blockade" Became a War: The Houthis and the Making of a Two-Strait Oil Crisis

What began in July as a symbolic naval blockade of Saudi Arabia has, by September, turned into a battlefield offensive that gives Yemen's Houthis real control over a second global chokepoint — and diplomats admit there is no fix for it without first ending the separate war between the United States and Iran.

By Zubair 9 min read Published Sep 18, 2026
Photo: Bergadder / Pixabay

On July 20, 2026, Yemen's Houthi movement — officially Ansar Allah — announced a naval blockade of Saudi Arabia, describing it as an "eye for an eye" response to a Saudi-backed strike on Sanaa airport. At the time, it read as a political declaration: a threat, backed by a group with a documented history of disrupting Red Sea shipping, but with no clear sense of whether it would ever be enforced.

Two months later, that question has been answered. By mid-September, Houthi forces had swept down Yemen's Red Sea coast, seized the port city of Mokha, taken islands near the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, and pushed to within 32 kilometers of Djibouti — home to major U.S. and international military bases. What was announced as a blockade has become a battlefield fact: the Houthis now physically control the coastline overlooking one of the world's most important shipping lanes, at the exact moment the other major chokepoint in the region, the Strait of Hormuz, remains effectively shut down by the ongoing U.S.-Iran war.

This is no longer a story about a rhetorical threat. It is a story about how two wars — one old, one new — have quietly merged into a single crisis for global energy markets, with Yemen's civilian population absorbing most of the damage.

Saudi Arabia's alternate oil route runs directly through the coastline Houthi forces now control.

From Declaration to Occupation

The chain of events is short but consequential. The July blockade announcement followed a Saudi-backed strike on Sanaa airport, intended to stop an Iranian aircraft carrying a Houthi delegation from landing; Houthi forces retaliated with missiles at Saudi Arabia's Abha airport. Fighting between Houthi and government forces then broke out around Hodeidah, straining a truce that had held, imperfectly, since 2022.

By September, this had escalated into what the Council on Foreign Relations' Global Conflict Tracker now classifies as a reignition of Yemen's twelve-year civil war. Houthi forces launched drone and missile strikes on Saudi's East-West pipeline — its main overland alternative for moving oil to the Red Sea — forcing a temporary shutdown. They struck the King Khalid airbase and a Saudi oil facility in Yanbu. Saudi Arabia responded with dozens of daily airstrikes on Hodeidah, Marib, and Taiz, and reportedly approached Egypt, France, Pakistan, and the United Kingdom for air-defense support after running low on missile interceptors.

The Houthis, for their part, insist their campaign is narrowly targeted. As Houthi political bureau member Mohammed al-Bukhaiti put it to the Associated Press, the underlying grievance is Saudi Arabia's own long-running blockade on Houthi-controlled territory, not a desire to reopen a global shipping war.

The Numbers Behind the Panic

The reason this matters far beyond Yemen's borders comes down to geography. The Strait of Hormuz has carried roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil and gas in peacetime; it has been largely closed to commercial traffic since the U.S.-Israel war on Iran began in late February, with ship-tracking data showing only a handful of vessels crossing on an average day, compared with well over a hundred before the war. Bab el-Mandeb, meanwhile, has carried somewhere between 5 and 7 percent of global oil output in recent data — but it is Saudi Arabia's main workaround for exporting crude via its Yanbu terminal, now handling roughly four times the volume it did a year ago as Riyadh routes oil away from Hormuz.

Put simply: Saudi Arabia built its Plan B around the very route the Houthis now threaten. Analysts have warned that a simultaneous closure of both straits could block up to a quarter of the world's oil and gas supply — a scenario that, until this year, would have sounded like a worst-case hypothetical.

Markets are already pricing in the strain. Brent crude has traded well above $100 a barrel, touching its highest levels since May, and U.S. retail diesel prices hit a record high in September. Qatari officials, who have positioned themselves as regional mediators, have been blunt about the stakes.

"The world is suffering enough with the closure of the strait of Hormuz, so we cannot also afford to add the Bab al-Mandab into that equation."
— Ibrahim al-Hashmi, Qatar Foreign Ministry

Two Wars, One Knot

What makes this crisis difficult to resolve is that it is not really one conflict — it is two, tangled together. Iran backs the Houthis and, according to Reuters reporting, has signaled it may lean on them as leverage in its own standoff with Washington over Hormuz. The Houthis, notably, have so far kept their attacks focused on Saudi-linked shipping rather than reviving the indiscriminate campaign against international vessels that they ran during the 2023–2024 Red Sea crisis — a restraint widely read as tied to a ceasefire the group struck with the United States, under which they agreed not to target U.S., Israeli, or general commercial shipping.

That restraint is precisely why Washington has been reluctant to get involved. President Trump has rebuffed Saudi requests for direct military support against the Houthis, and U.S. officials have reportedly met Houthi representatives in Oman, where the group pledged to uphold last year's ceasefire. With Trump facing political headwinds over rising energy prices ahead of November's midterms, opening a second American military front in Yemen holds little appeal — even for a close ally.

That leaves diplomacy fragmented across multiple, unsynchronized tracks: Oman-hosted talks on a Hormuz shipping corridor, Chinese-mediated discussions between Iran and Saudi Arabia, and Qatari efforts to prevent the Yemen conflict from compounding the Hormuz crisis. As Reuters has reported, no formal U.S.-Iran negotiations have taken place since an interim agreement collapsed within weeks of being reached in June — meaning the piece of the puzzle most likely to actually unlock Yemen's crisis, a broader U.S.-Iran settlement, currently has no active negotiating channel at all.

The Cost Measured in People, Not Barrels

Lost in the market analysis is a humanitarian toll that was already severe before this escalation. The UN's humanitarian office estimates more than 125,000 Yemenis have been displaced by the latest fighting, most within Yemen itself, while thousands more have crossed the Bab el-Mandeb — literally, "Gate of Tears" — by small boat to reach Djibouti. Yemen's Saudi-backed Health Ministry says roughly 150 civilians have been killed by Houthi attacks since early September; Saudi Arabia has reported its own first civilian death from intercepted drone debris. The UN's humanitarian appeal for Yemen remains only about 20 percent funded.

This is not new suffering so much as an old wound reopening. Yemen was already the poorest country in the Arab world before 2014, and the civil war's toll — by some estimates over 150,000 deaths and hundreds of thousands more from indirect causes like malnutrition and collapsed health infrastructure — made it, by UN measures, the world's worst humanitarian crisis of the past decade.

The Counterargument: Is This Really a "Crisis" Yet?

It's worth taking seriously the case that this story is being overdramatized. Shipping-tracking firm Windward reported that daily transits through Bab el-Mandeb dipped from 35 to 25 after the Houthi advance began — but had already recovered to 45 by mid-September, suggesting the strait is not, in practice, closed. The Houthis have also been explicit and consistent that their targeting criteria are limited to Saudi-linked vessels, not a blanket campaign against global shipping, which is a meaningful difference from the 2023–2024 episode that triggered mass rerouting around Africa.

The counterargument has real weight. But it likely understates the risk of drift. ACLED analyst Valentin d'Hauthuille has warned that the Houthis could still choose to widen their definition of "Saudi-linked" shipping to increase pressure on both Riyadh and the global economy — precisely the kind of scope creep that turned the 2023 campaign from a narrow political statement into a multi-month disruption of nearly 15 percent of global seaborne trade. Add to that the fact that the group now holds physical territory along the strait rather than simply operating missiles and drones from a distance, and the theoretical capacity for escalation is arguably higher than it was two years ago, even if current behavior is restrained.

What to Watch Next

Three variables will likely determine which direction this goes:

  1. The battle for Marib — Yemen's oil-rich central province and the last major stronghold of the Saudi-backed government, which the Houthis have targeted before without success. A Houthi breakthrough there would mark a serious shift in the civil war's momentum.
  2. Whether the fragmented diplomatic tracks converge — Oman, China-Iran, and Qatar are currently running separate, unsynchronized negotiations. Resolving Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb in isolation from each other looks increasingly unrealistic.
  3. Whether the Houthis hold the line on targeting only Saudi-linked shipping — this single distinction is currently the main thing standing between a regional oil squeeze and a full-blown global shipping crisis.

For now, the blockade that began as a single sentence in a July statement has become something much harder to walk back: a fact on the ground, held by an armed force, along one of the two arteries the world's oil supply depends on most.
